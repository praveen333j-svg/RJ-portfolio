# Australia's AI Governance Moment: Between Canberra, Washington and Brussels

*Independent policy analysis | Updated 4 August 2026 | Estimated reading time: 14 minutes*

> Key message. Australia does not need to choose between the European and American approaches to AI governance. It needs a distinctly Australian model — clear ownership, practical implementation, strong safeguards for high-risk use, room for innovation — built the way Australia builds institutions that last: as a delivery program with an accountable owner, a structure and a timeline.

If an AI system harms an Australian tomorrow — misprices an insurance claim, embeds discrimination in a hiring decision, denies a benefit a person was entitled to — which regulator owns the failure? Who answers to Parliament?

Today the honest answer is: it depends. The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner might see the privacy dimension. The ACCC might see the consumer harm. APRA might see the prudential exposure. Each would act within its mandate. None would own the system that caused the harm.

That is the real AI governance question in front of Canberra. Not whether to regulate artificial intelligence — Australia already does, in more places than the public debate acknowledges — but whether anyone owns the risks that sit above any single regulator’s mandate.

The policy balance is genuinely difficult. Move too slowly, and Australians are exposed to harms that spread faster than traditional regulatory cycles can respond. Move too aggressively, and Australia constrains investment, adoption and domestic capability before the benefits are realised.

And Australia’s position is distinctive. It is not the United States, with a frontier-model industrial base and a venture-scale technology ecosystem. It is not the European Union, with the market size to export regulatory norms globally. Australia is predominantly an adopter of AI built and governed elsewhere — which makes its strategic task different: build a governance model that protects public trust, enables responsible adoption, and gives government and industry an operating framework they can actually implement.

So the question this article answers is:

What kind of AI governance model does Australia need — and, just as importantly, how does it get built?

The argument in one line: Australia’s governance gap is ownership, not activity — and closing it is a delivery program, not a drafting exercise. That single frame carries every section that follows.

### 1. Australia Is Already Governing AI — Just Not as One System

A common assumption in the public debate is that Australia has not begun regulating artificial intelligence. That is not accurate.

Australia already governs many AI-related risks through privacy, consumer protection, competition, anti-discrimination, cyber security, workplace, prudential, health and online safety frameworks.1 The current model is not a regulatory vacuum. It is a distributed system built on existing legal and institutional machinery, consistent with Australia’s long-standing preference for technology-neutral regulation: apply enduring legal principles to new technologies wherever possible, rather than writing a new statute for every technological wave.1

The strength of this model is specialist competence. The OAIC understands privacy, and has issued guidance on both developing generative AI models and deploying commercial AI products.2 APRA has written to banks, insurers and superannuation trustees on AI risk governance and operational resilience.3 The TGA regulates AI-enabled medical device software by intended purpose, not by technology.4 The ACCC pursues misleading AI claims and market conduct. That expertise took decades to build. It should not be displaced lightly.

From what I observe, the federal government is currently relying on that machinery. The National AI Plan, launched in December 2025, emphasises existing, largely technology-neutral legal frameworks, regulators’ existing expertise and voluntary guidance for responsible adoption.5 The September 2024 proposals paper, with its proposed definition of high-risk AI and ten proposed guardrails, remains an important reference point; the Plan retains an explicit safety goal alongside its growth agenda.6

Where Australia has moved furthest is inside government itself. The Digital Transformation Agency’s updated policy for the responsible use of AI took effect on 15 December 2025 for non-corporate Commonwealth entities. It strengthens the requirements for accountable officials, internal registers and impact assessments, with requirements phasing in through 2026.7 This matters because government does not simply deploy technology. It exercises public authority.

So the accurate description of Australia today: no single AI Act, but an increasingly dense governance architecture. Plenty of activity. The open question is whether activity is the same thing as ownership.

### 2. The Gap Is Ownership, Not Activity

What are we actually trying to do here? Strip the debate back to first principles and the answer is short: when an AI system causes harm, someone identifiable must be accountable for detecting it, containing it and fixing it — and Australians must know whose door to knock on.

Measured against that test, the distributed model may have a structural gap. Each regulator sees its slice; no single body is assigned ownership of the cross-cutting layer. Where a harm falls between mandates, accountability can become difficult for the public to locate and for government to coordinate.

And the exposure is not narrow. AI risk will emerge across health, finance, employment, education, digital platforms, cyber security, critical infrastructure, social services and democratic processes. Some of those risks will be clearly owned by a regulator. Others will sit awkwardly between mandates.

Australia does have coordination structures — but coordination is not the same as ownership. The Digital Platform Regulators Forum convenes the ACCC, the ACMA, the eSafety Commissioner and the OAIC, and its principal output is working papers.8 The DTA’s AI Review Committee, launched in June 2026, provides expert, non-binding advice to agencies on high-risk, sensitive, novel or complex use cases.9 These mechanisms are valuable, but they do not create a single accountable owner for cross-cutting AI risk. That is the institutional question this article explores.

There is also a strategic cost to leaving the gap open. If Canberra does not define Australia’s operating model, the practical rules of AI adoption will be set elsewhere — by US technology providers, European compliance expectations, global cloud platforms and multinational procurement terms. A slower domestic legislative path does not preserve maximum policy freedom. In practice, it transfers the pen. Australia is not choosing from a blank page. It is choosing whether to remain a downstream rule-taker or to build a model of its own.

The technology does not change any of this. AI never absorbs accountability. Humans do.

### 3. Washington and Brussels Are Lessons, Not Templates

The two most influential global models reflect different opening questions.

The European Union starts by asking how to protect citizens. Its AI Act is a comprehensive, risk-based framework: certain uses prohibited, high-risk systems subject to stringent obligations, transparency and human oversight treated as design requirements rather than optional extras.10 The philosophy is explicit — public trust is a condition for sustainable adoption, not a by-product of it.

The United States starts by asking how to maintain leadership. Its model is decentralised: executive direction, voluntary standards, sector regulators, state initiatives and market-led implementation. The philosophy is equally explicit — preserve speed, attract investment, keep the strategic advantage.

| European Union | United States |
| --- | --- |
| Protect citizens first | Promote innovation first |
| Comprehensive risk-based legislation | Decentralised, sector-led governance |
| Human rights, transparency and accountability focus | Competitiveness, investment and speed focus |
| Trust enables innovation | Innovation builds capability |

Neither is simply right or wrong, and neither should be imported wholesale. A purely American approach leaves too many harms unresolved for a country whose legal culture prizes fairness, consumer protection and institutional trust. A purely European approach imposes an administrative weight that smaller Australian firms cannot absorb. Australia needs the discipline of the first without its weight, and the dynamism of the second without its gaps.

### 4. The Brussels Effect: Sovereignty Is a Delivery Capability

There is a complication for anyone assuming Canberra can take its time choosing: part of the choice may already be made.

The Brussels Effect describes the European Union’s ability to shape global business practice beyond its borders. Because the EU is one of the world’s largest markets, companies frequently apply European standards globally rather than run multiple compliance frameworks in parallel. GDPR is the proven example: organisations worldwide adopted GDPR-inspired privacy controls because maintaining several privacy regimes became more expensive than maintaining one.

The same dynamic is emerging for AI. The EU AI Act already applies to organisations that place AI systems into the European market, wherever they are headquartered.10 For multinational technology providers, one global governance framework is simpler than dozens of regional variations. Which produces a practical consequence for Australia: even if Parliament never legislates an equivalent of the EU AI Act, Australian organisations may operate under many of its principles anyway — because their customers demand it, their suppliers require it, their technology providers embed it globally, and their international partners expect it.

This lands harder in Australia than in most economies, because Australia is predominantly a consumer and adopter of AI designed, developed and governed offshore. The most influential AI platforms inside Australian organisations are not governed from Australia.

That reframes what sovereignty means. Sovereignty is not only the power to write domestic rules. It is the institutional capability to interpret, adapt and implement rules in the national interest — and without that capability, Australia retains policy independence on paper while operationally absorbing standards designed in Brussels, Washington and Silicon Valley. Rule-making is a right. Rule-shaping is a capability.

### 5. A Spine, Not a Super-Regulator

If the gap is ownership, two remedies present themselves immediately. Both fail.

The first is a single AI mega-regulator that absorbs the expertise of the privacy, competition, prudential, health, online safety and workplace regulators. That replaces coordination failure with competence failure. A greenfield body would take years to build and a decade to acquire the domain depth APRA, the TGA and the OAIC already hold — and the transition window would be the period of greatest risk.

The second is another voluntary coordination forum. Australia already has those, and Section 2 described their structural limit: forums analyse problems without owning outcomes. Scaling the model up reproduces the defect at greater expense — everyone contributes staff, nobody contributes accountability. In delivery terms, it is a steering committee without an empowered delivery owner. A structure whose record is meetings, not outcomes.

I propose a model that combines three instincts: European-style safeguards for high-risk use, American-style encouragement of innovation, and Australian-style delivery pragmatism. Its missing ingredient is a regulatory spine: a binding horizontal framework for high-risk and general-purpose AI, a clear owner for frontier-model technical oversight, and a statutory mechanism for escalating gaps that fall between existing regulators.

A practical Australian model has three layers:

| Layer | Role in the model |
| --- | --- |
| Sector regulators | Retain domain enforcement: privacy, consumer, prudential, safety, health, cyber and sector-specific obligations. APRA on prudential model risk; the OAIC on privacy; the ACCC on consumer harm; the TGA on medical devices. |
| Statutory AI coordination authority | Owns the horizontal layer: sets binding cross-sector standards, maintains a gap-escalation mechanism, and reports unresolved systemic risks to ministers, Parliament and the public. |
| Australian AI Safety Institute | Upgraded for the narrow slice no existing regulator owns: frontier-model capability, systemic risk, testing and evidence-based advice — building on its current technical and policy-support role within the Department of Industry.11 |

Australia has already seen what happens when accountability is distributed but not owned. The Robodebt experience demonstrated how long a systemic failure can persist when responsibility is fragmented across institutions. AI creates the same governance risk at greater speed and scale. If an AI system harms a person, distorts a market or undermines trust in public decision-making, Australians should not have to work out which regulatory door to knock on.

AI governance is not only a legal problem. It is a delivery problem.

### 6. Treat the Build as a Delivery Program — Because It Is One

A practical route to building that spine would not start from a blank organisational chart or wait for consensus to emerge from forums. It would apply a machinery-of-government pattern Australia has used before: an interagency taskforce that designs and stands up a statutory authority, built from existing capability rather than recruited from scratch.

Ministers would commission the taskforce with formal terms of reference: regulatory capability drawn from the OAIC, the ACCC, APRA, ASIC and the eSafety Commissioner; technical depth from the AI Safety Institute; and an operating playbook from the DTA — the only body in Australia currently running a mandatory AI governance regime of registers, impact assessments and accountable officials.7 Critically, the terms of reference would make the participating agency heads jointly accountable for delivering the framework, the organisational structure and the timeline. Accountability conferred from above, not volunteered sideways, is what separates a delivery program from another forum.

The destination is a statutory AI authority, legislated by Parliament, staffed initially through secondment and then by permanent transfer with its own appropriation. The sequencing matters. Seconded staff are recalled the moment a home agency faces a crisis, and a body without its own funding line hollows out within two budget cycles. The authority’s head would answer to Parliament for the horizontal layer that currently has no owner.

This is not an untested proposition. The Australian Cyber Security Centre was assembled from capability across ASD, the AFP, ACIC and ASIO. The National Anti-Corruption Commission moved from implementation taskforce to operations in roughly eighteen months. The eSafety Commissioner itself was carved out of the ACMA. These examples suggest that an eighteen-to-twenty-four-month build is a plausible planning assumption with political will; it is not a forecast.

And it carries the disciplines any serious delivery program carries: an accountable owner, a defined structure, a timeline, and a go/no-go decision — rather than a permanent consultation.

### The Question Canberra Must Be Able to Answer

Return to the opening question. An AI system harms an Australian tomorrow. Which regulator owns the failure? Who answers to Parliament?

Under today’s settings, the answer is a diagram. Under the model described here, it is a name.

The comparative lesson of Washington and Brussels is that legal form matters less than ownership. The EU codified a comprehensive risk-based framework; the United States has relied more heavily on decentralised, sector-led coordination; both remain institutionally plural. Australia’s opportunity is to treat regulatory design as a delivery program.

Frameworks do not fail because they are distributed. They fail because no one is accountable when they do.

> Australia does not need to become Europe. It does not need to become the United States. It needs to become more deliberate about being Australia: clear on accountability, practical in delivery and confident enough to set the rules by which AI earns public trust.
